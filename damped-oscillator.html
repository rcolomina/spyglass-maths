The damped oscillator, hands on

One equation, three places it lives: a classroom, an aircraft and a flight computer. Each panel below lets you change the parameters and watch the solution respond.

ẍ + 2ζωn ẋ + ωn2 x = ωn2 u,   x(0) = ẋ(0) = 0,   u = 1

1 · One number shapes the response

The damping ratio ζ decides everything about the shape of the step response; ωn only stretches time. Both are read off the two roots of λ2 + 2ζωnλ + ωn2 = 0, the poles. Below ζ = 1 they sit on a circle of radius ωn and the response rings; at ζ = 1 they collide; above it they split along the real axis and the response creeps.

2 · A pitch autopilot is the same equation

Hold the nose of an aircraft at a commanded pitch angle θref. The simplified pitch dynamics are θ̈ = Mq θ̇ + Mδ δ, and the autopilot moves the elevator with a PD law δ = Kp(θref − θ) − Kd θ̇. Substituting gives the oscillator again, with

ωn2 = Kp Mδ,    2ζωn = Kd Mδ − Mq,    Mδ = 10 s−2, Mq = −1 s−1

Choosing the gains is choosing ζ and ωn. Drag the dot in the gain plane or use the sliders. Then pick the fast design: it settles in 0.75 s instead of 2 s, but asks for 32° of elevator at the start, more than a real surface can give. Tick the limit to clip the deflection at ±25° and compare.

3 · On a flight computer, the sample time matters

A computer runs the loop every h seconds. Forward Euler replaces the derivative by a difference quotient, xk+1 = xk + h(Axk + Bu), and is stable only while every hλ stays inside the disk |1 + hλ| < 1. For an underdamped loop that means

h < hmax = 2ζ / ωn

The exact zero-order-hold update xk+1 = eAhxk + Γu has eigenvalues ehλ, always inside the unit circle, so it is stable for every h. This panel uses the gains you chose above (without the elevator limit). Slide h past hmax and watch Euler leave the circle.
